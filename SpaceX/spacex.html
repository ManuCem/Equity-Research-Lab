SpaceX (SPCX): a first look at the accounts

A basic accounting analysis built only from SpaceX's SEC documents: the S-1/A prospectus (years 2023-2025 and Q1 2026) and the Q2 and Q3 2026 earnings releases. US dollars; data up to 30 September 2026.

Personal learning project, not investment advice. This page shows facts, ratios and questions only: no buy/sell view, no price target, no valuation.
The short version. SpaceX runs three businesses: rockets (Space), Starlink internet (Connectivity) and artificial intelligence (AI, renamed Super Intelligence or SI in Q3 2026). Revenue is growing very fast: $27.1bn in the first nine months of 2026, already more than all of 2025 ($18.7bn). Q3 2026 was the only quarter with a profit among the 6 in these files. But the company spends far more on new equipment than its operations bring in, and that gap was paid for by its June 2026 stock market listing (IPO) and by borrowing.

1. How big is the business, and where does the revenue come from?

Revenue (money earned from customers for what was delivered in the period) grew from $10.4bn in 2023 to $18.7bn in 2025. 2026 is a step change: $14.6bn in Q3 alone, 161% more than in Q3 2025. SpaceX reports three segments (business units reported separately). In 2025 Connectivity (Starlink) was the biggest, at 61% of revenue. By Q3 2026 AI / SI had become the largest, at 52%, driven mainly by cloud services agreements (renting computing power to other companies), Grok, and Cursor, an AI coding company bought in August 2026 ($608m of revenue since then).

2. Where does the money go?

Each bar shows the main costs for every $100 of revenue. Cost of revenue (the direct cost of delivering what was sold: hardware, launches, data-centre running costs) fell from 59% of revenue in 2023 to 38% in Q3 2026. Research and development (R&D) (spending on new products such as Starship and AI models) is the cost that grew most: from 20% of revenue in 2023 to 46% in 2025. SG&A (selling, general and administrative costs: sales, marketing, offices, management) is smaller, about 12%. Interest expense (what the company pays its lenders) was 10% of revenue in 2025. When a bar is taller than the 100% line, these costs were bigger than revenue. That is true for every period shown except Q3 2026, when they fell to 85% because revenue grew much faster than costs.

2023 includes a $3.8bn impairment (a write-down because an asset lost value): the Twitter brand after its rename to X. "9M" = January-September.

3. Is it profitable, and is that improving?

Three margins (profit as a share of revenue) tell the story. Gross margin (revenue minus cost of revenue, as % of revenue) rose from 41% in 2023 to 62% in Q3 2026. Operating margin (what is left after ALL operating costs, including R&D and SG&A) was negative in 4 of the 6 quarters shown and reached 19% in Q3 2026, the highest in these files. Net income (the bottom line, after interest, other gains and losses, and tax) was $4.4bn in Q3 2026, larger than operating income ($2.8bn) because of $1.0bn of other income, $873m of interest income on the cash pile, and a $390m tax benefit. January-September 2026 as a whole still shows a net loss of $413m.

Q1 2026's very low net margin includes a $1.9bn "other expense", mostly a $1.5bn loss from repaying debt early (S-1/A cash flow statement, page F-67).

Management says (Q3 call, not a filing)Q3's gross margin included one-off benefits (roughly 3 percentage points in Connectivity, mainly tariff refunds); management expects Q4 2026 gross margin to look more like Q2's (55%).

4. Profit vs cash: is the company generating or using cash?

Profit and cash are not the same thing. Operating cash flow (cash collected from customers minus cash paid to run the business) was $13.1bn in January-September 2026, although that period shows a net loss of $413m. Why? Big costs such as depreciation (spreading the cost of rockets, satellites and computers over their useful life) and share-based compensation (paying staff in shares) reduce profit but use no cash; and customers paid $4.9bn in advance (deferred revenue: cash received for work not done yet). Then the company spent $48.1bn on capital expenditures (capex): data centres, satellites, launch sites. That is 3.7 times its operating cash flow. Free cash flow (operating cash flow minus capex) was -$35.0bn. So SpaceX is using cash, not generating it, and has been since 2024.

5. How much does it owe, and how much cash does it hold?

On 30 September 2026 SpaceX held $89.9bn of cash and marketable securities (short-term investments that can be sold quickly) against $38.8bn of debt and finance leases (borrowed money, plus long leases that work like loans). That leaves net cash of $51.0bn. At the end of 2025, before the IPO, the two were about even (net cash $1.9bn). In June 2026 the IPO raised about $85.7bn and the company also sold $25bn of bonds. Debt still rose by only $9.1bn between March and June, because older loans were repaid: debt repayments were $39.8bn in January-September 2026.

Interest expense was $2.0bn in January-September 2026, partly offset by $1.4bn of interest income earned on the cash.

6. Six ratios in plain English

Ratios turn the statements into a few comparable numbers. Margins (first three) show how much of each dollar of revenue is kept. Capex / operating cash flow shows how much investment exceeds what the operations bring in. The current ratio and debt-to-equity are balance-sheet ratios: they compare what the company owns and owes at a date. Both improved sharply after the IPO.

From adjusted EBITDA to net income, step by step (Q3 2026)

EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. Adjusted EBITDA also leaves out other items management chooses (here share-based compensation, acquisition costs and restructuring). It is a non-GAAP measure: GAAP are the official US accounting rules, and this number is not defined by them, so each company builds its own. Management likes it because it strips out non-cash and one-off items; critics point out that those costs are real. The waterfall starts from Q3 2026 adjusted EBITDA of $8.8bn and takes each excluded item back in, until it reaches the $4.4bn of net income in the income statement.

Grey = subtotals; orange = takes money away; blue = adds money. Source: Q3 release, reconciliation table (page 10) and income statement (page 9).

  1. Depreciation & amortization (-$3.6bn): the yearly share of the cost of GPUs, satellites and rockets. No cash leaves this quarter (the cash left when they were bought, as capex), but the assets wear out, so it is a real cost.
  2. Share-based compensation (-$1.1bn): staff paid in shares. No cash, but existing shareholders own a smaller slice.
  3. Share-based compensation from acquisitions (-$1.2bn): shares granted to keep Cursor's staff. The release says $23.4bn of such awards will be expensed over the next few years, so this item will come back.
  4. Acquisition-related costs (-$8m) and restructuring (-$3m): lawyers' and advisers' fees for the Cursor deal, and costs of reorganising the business.
  5. = Operating income ($2.8bn): matches the income statement exactly. This is where GAAP profit from the core business stands.
  6. Interest expense (-$675m) and interest income (+$873m): paid on debt, earned on the cash and securities raised in the IPO.
  7. Other income (+$1.0bn): gains outside the core business ($561m of it from a related party). What it is, and whether it repeats, is explained in the 10-Q, not in these files.
  8. Income tax (+$390m): a tax benefit this quarter, so tax added to profit instead of reducing it. = Net income $4.4bn.

Open questions a beginner could investigate

  1. What did Q4 2025 look like? It is not reported on its own here. Work it out: full year 2025 minus January-September 2025 (both are in the workbook). Was Q4 2025 profitable?
  2. Why was net income bigger than operating income in Q3 2026? Find what the $1.0bn of "other income" and the $390m tax benefit were (the Q3 10-Q notes). Are they likely to repeat?
  3. How much of the Q3 gross margin jump will last? Gross margin went from 55.3% in Q2 to 62.4% in Q3; management says part was one-off. Which segment drove it?
  4. How will the cash gap be funded? Capex was $48.1bn in January-September and management expects $35-40bn more in Q4 alone, against $13.1bn of operating cash flow. How long does $89.9bn of cash and securities last at that pace, and what new debt could be needed?
  5. Where did the $25bn bond money go? Debt rose only $9.1bn from March to June 2026. Which loans were repaid, and why was there a $1.5bn loss on repaying debt early in Q1?
  6. What is the $48.4bn of goodwill? Goodwill (the part of a purchase price above the fair value of what was bought) rose from $11.6bn in June, mostly from Cursor ($42.5bn purchase price). What would an impairment mean, as with the Twitter brand in 2023?
  7. How big is share-based compensation becoming? $3.8bn in January-September 2026. Compare it with operating income and with adjusted EBITDA.
  8. Why is deferred revenue growing? It reached $17.5bn at 30 September 2026 (current + non-current). Who is paying in advance, and what does that mean for future revenue and cash?

Glossary

Revenue
Money earned from customers for what the company delivered in the period (not necessarily cash received yet).
Segment
A business unit the company reports on separately: here Space (rockets), Connectivity (Starlink) and AI / SI.
Cost of revenue
The direct cost of delivering what was sold, such as hardware, launch costs and data-centre running costs.
Gross profit / margin
Revenue minus cost of revenue; as a % of revenue it is the gross margin.
R&D
Research and development: spending on new products such as Starship and new AI models.
SG&A
Selling, general and administrative costs: sales, marketing, offices, management and support staff.
Operating income
Revenue minus all operating costs: what the core business earned before interest and tax.
Net income
The bottom line: profit (or loss) after every cost, interest, other gains and losses, and tax.
Depreciation & amortization
Spreading the cost of long-lived assets (rockets, satellites, computers) over the years they are used; a cost that uses no cash that year.
Share-based compensation
Paying employees in shares or options; it is a real cost to shareholders but not a cash payment.
Operating cash flow
Cash actually collected from customers minus cash paid to run the business in the period.
Capex
Capital expenditures: cash spent on long-lived assets such as data centres, satellites and launch pads.
Free cash flow
Operating cash flow minus capex: the cash left over (or missing) after investing.
Deferred revenue
Cash received from customers for work not done yet; it becomes revenue later.
Marketable securities
Short-term investments (like Treasury bills) that can be sold quickly for cash.
Debt and finance leases
Borrowed money plus long leases that work like loans (the company effectively owns the asset).
Shareholders' equity
What would be left for shareholders if all liabilities were paid: assets minus liabilities (and minus preferred stock shown outside equity).
Redeemable convertible preferred stock
Shares sold to early investors that could be bought back or converted; shown between liabilities and equity until the IPO converted them.
Goodwill
The part of an acquisition price above the fair value of what was bought; it is written down (impaired) if the deal disappoints.
Impairment
A write-down when an asset is worth less than its book value (for example the Twitter brand in 2023).
Adjusted EBITDA (non-GAAP)
Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization, also excluding items management picks; not defined by GAAP, the official US accounting rules.
Backlog
Signed contracts not yet delivered and not yet counted as revenue.
YTD / 9M
Year-to-date; 9M = the first nine months (January-September).
